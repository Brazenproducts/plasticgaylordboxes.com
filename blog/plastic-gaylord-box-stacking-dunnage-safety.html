Safe Stacking and Dunnage Practices for Plastic Gaylord Boxes

Plasticgaylordboxes Editorial • October 2026

Plastic gaylord boxes earn their keep by stacking high and cycling often, but both benefits depend on disciplined handling. A collapsed stack damages product, endangers workers, and erases the cost advantage of reusable packaging. This How-To gives warehouse supervisors a practical framework for stacking, dunnaging, and inspecting plastic gaylord boxes from Elipacko so that the containers perform to their rated capacity shift after shift.

Start With the Rated Loads, Not a Guess

Every molded or corrugated PP gaylord has three published limits: static stacking load, dynamic load during movement, and racking load for unsupported spans. Post these numbers at the receiving and storage areas. The single most common failure in the field is stacking to a visually comfortable height rather than to the engineered static load. Calculate maximum stack count by dividing the rated static load of the bottom container by the loaded weight of a single filled box, then round down and build in a safety margin.

Match the Box to the Right Pallet

A gaylord is only as stable as what sits beneath it. Deck boards that are too widely spaced let the base flex and concentrate stress at the corners. Pair each container with compatible plastic pallets rated for the same dynamic load, with a deck pattern that fully supports the box footprint. Confirm that the pallet top deck matches the box base dimensions so there is no overhang, which creates a shear point under compression.

Use Dunnage to Distribute Load Evenly

When product inside the box is dense or irregular, a flat layer pad or divider board across the top of each container spreads the next box's weight across the full rim rather than onto high spots. For partial loads, dunnage prevents the upper box from sagging into the void below. Keep these principles in mind:

  • Use full-footprint layer pads between stacked boxes carrying point loads
  • Never stack a heavier loaded box on top of a lighter one
  • Fill voids so walls are not asked to carry what the contents should
  • Keep lids or covers seated so rims transfer load as designed

Build Stable Stacks and Travel Lanes

Column stacking (boxes aligned corner over corner) preserves far more compression strength than interlocked or offset patterns, because vertical walls carry load best in pure compression. Align each box directly over the one below. Keep aisle clearances wide enough that forklift masts do not clip stacks, and mark maximum stack-height lines on racking uprights so operators have a visible limit.

Inspect on Every Cycle

Reusable containers accumulate wear. Train receiving staff to pull any box showing stress whitening at the corners, cracked rims, warped bases, or delamination in twin-wall panels. A quick visual check at each return point catches compromised units before they end up at the bottom of a tall stack. Containers that fail inspection should be removed from the fleet pool, not quietly returned to service.

Account for Temperature Effects

Polypropylene stiffens in cold and softens in heat, so rated loads assume a temperature range. In freezer or cold-chain operations, derate stack height if the manufacturer specifies reduced capacity at low temperatures. Near heat sources or in hot yards, watch for creep in long-term static storage. Make Safety Repeatable

Document your stacking rules in a one-page standard work sheet, post it at every storage zone, and audit it during routine safety walks. The payoff is tangible: predictable storage density, fewer damaged loads, and a container fleet that reaches its full rated service life. Safe stacking is not a constraint on throughput; it is what makes high-density, high-cycle gaylord programs sustainable over the long run.
